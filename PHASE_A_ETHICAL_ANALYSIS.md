# Phase A — Ethical Analysis of Synthetic Media

## 1. Reflection on Task 6

In Task 6, I created synthetic audio using ElevenLabs and then used the improved audio with a stock avatar in D-ID to create a synthetic video. The purpose of the experiment was not to impersonate a real person, and the information presented in the media was based on data I had previously verified.

What surprised me most was how quickly the synthetic audio became convincing. My first ElevenLabs attempt sounded somewhat robotic and had limited emotion, but after changing settings such as speed, stability, and style exaggeration, the second attempt sounded much more natural. To me, the improved version no longer had an obvious robotic quality.

The D-ID video produced a different result. The lip-sync was very convincing, but the avatar had minimal facial expression and no noticeable hand movements. This made the visual component easier to recognize as synthetic even though the audio itself sounded natural.

I also tested a 20-second portion of the improved audio using Hive's AI-generated audio detector. Although the audio sounded convincing to me, Hive identified it as AI-generated speech with 99.1% confidence. This showed me that something can appear authentic to a human listener while still containing characteristics that an automated system can detect.

The ethical concern that stands out to me now is how accessible this capability is. I was able to create a convincing synthetic voice and combine it with a realistic talking avatar without advanced technical knowledge. My project was harmless because the information was truthful, the avatar was generic, and the synthetic nature of the content was disclosed. However, the same process could be used very differently if someone changed the information, removed the disclosure, or used another person's identity without permission.

### Truth Axis

In Task 6, the synthetic voice and avatar presented information that I had already verified. A different ethical situation would arise if someone used the same tools to communicate false information.

For example, imagine someone creates a synthetic video of a university athletic director announcing that a head coach has been fired because of misconduct. The video uses a realistic AI-generated version of the athletic director's voice and appearance and is posted online without a synthetic-media disclosure. The announcement is completely fabricated, but students, athletes, parents, and reporters begin sharing it before the university can respond.

The technology used in this scenario is not very different from what I used in Task 6. A script can be converted into convincing speech and paired with a talking avatar relatively quickly. The major ethical difference is that the synthetic representation is being used to give false information the appearance and authority of a real speaker.

The consequences could continue even after the university proves that the video is fake. People who only saw the original clip might never see the correction, and copies could continue circulating. This makes truthfulness especially important with synthetic media because realistic delivery can make fabricated information appear credible before a viewer has an opportunity to verify it.

My Task 6 experience showed me that the realism of synthetic media cannot be treated as evidence that the underlying message is true. The improved ElevenLabs recording sounded natural to me even though I knew it was generated by AI. If the information in that recording had also been false, the realistic voice could have made the false claim more persuasive rather than making it easier to recognize as fabricated.

### Consent Axis

In Task 6, I used a generic stock avatar rather than copying the identity of a real person. The ethical situation changes significantly when someone's real voice or likeness is used without permission.

For example, imagine a student clones a professor's voice and creates a synthetic video in which the professor appears to announce that an upcoming exam has been canceled. The professor never made the announcement and never gave permission for their voice or likeness to be used. Students who believe the video might stop preparing for the exam or share the announcement with other students.

The main ethical issue is no longer only whether the information is true or false. The person's identity itself has been used without consent. A recognizable voice or face carries credibility because audiences associate it with a particular person. Using those characteristics without permission allows the creator to borrow that person's identity and authority.

My Task 6 experience showed me how easily a synthetic voice can become convincing after only a few adjustments. If similar technology were used to imitate a real person, viewers might reasonably believe that person actually said something they never said. The person being represented could then be forced to prove that the content was fake.

Consent therefore represents an important ethical boundary for synthetic media. Even if synthetic technology has legitimate uses, a person's recognizable voice, face, or other identifying characteristics should not be used without their knowledge and permission.

### Context Axis

In Task 6, I clearly labeled my audio and video as AI-generated. However, a disclosure only works if it remains attached to the content when other people encounter it.

For example, imagine a university creates a clearly labeled synthetic video using an AI avatar to explain a new campus policy. The original video includes an AI-generated disclosure, but someone downloads the video, removes the disclosure, and reposts a short clip on social media. The repost makes it appear that the avatar is a real university representative speaking directly to students.

The underlying information may still be accurate, but the context has changed. Viewers of the reposted version no longer have the information needed to understand how the content was created. A person could also edit or caption the clip in a way that changes how viewers interpret the original message.

This creates an ethical problem because the original creator cannot completely control how synthetic media will be presented after publication. Labels, watermarks, and metadata can help provide transparency, but they can potentially be cropped, removed, or lost when content is copied between platforms.

My Task 6 project showed me why disclosure is important, but it also made me realize that disclosure alone cannot guarantee responsible use. Synthetic media should be created with the expectation that it may eventually appear outside its original context.

### Scale Axis

In Task 6, I created only a few synthetic-media artifacts and spent time reviewing each result. The ethical risks become much greater when the same technology is used to generate content at a large scale.

For example, imagine someone creates hundreds of synthetic videos about a university during a crisis. The videos use different AI-generated speakers and contain a mixture of accurate information, misleading claims, and completely false announcements. These videos are then automatically posted across multiple social media accounts.

Even if individual videos are eventually identified as synthetic, the volume of content could make verification difficult. Students, employees, journalists, and university officials would have to determine which announcements were legitimate while new synthetic content continued to appear.

My Task 6 experience showed me that producing convincing synthetic media does not necessarily require advanced technical skills or a large amount of time. Once a working process has been established, the same process could potentially be repeated many times.

Scale therefore changes the ethical problem. The risk is not limited to whether one person believes one fake video. Large-scale synthetic-media production could overwhelm people's ability to verify information and reduce trust even in legitimate communications. This makes responsible governance important before synthetic-media tools are used widely within an organization.

## 3. Mitigation Landscape

Synthetic media creates risks that cannot be addressed by a single solution. Several approaches can reduce these risks, but each has limitations.

### Disclosure and Labeling

Disclosure tells viewers when content has been generated or modified using AI. This can include labels, watermarks, spoken disclosures, or on-screen text. In Task 6, I clearly identified my artifacts as AI-generated.

Disclosure is useful because it gives the audience important context before they evaluate the content. However, it depends on the label remaining attached to the artifact. A disclosure can potentially be cropped from a video, removed during editing, or lost when content is reposted. Therefore, disclosure improves transparency but cannot guarantee that every future viewer will know the content is synthetic.

### Provenance and Content Credentials

Provenance systems attempt to preserve information about where digital content came from and how it was created. Technologies such as C2PA and content credentials can attach information about the origin and editing history of media.

These systems could help audiences verify whether media came from a trusted source. However, provenance information may not always survive downloading, editing, re-encoding, or reposting. It also depends on platforms and tools supporting the same standards. Provenance can provide useful evidence of authenticity, but the absence of provenance does not automatically prove that something is fake.

### Automated Detection

AI detectors attempt to identify characteristics associated with synthetic media. In Task 6, I tested a 20-second excerpt of my improved ElevenLabs recording with Hive. Although the voice sounded natural to me, Hive identified it as AI-generated speech with 99.1% confidence.

This demonstrated that automated detection can identify synthetic characteristics that may not be obvious to a human listener. However, my test also showed a practical limitation because the interface only accepted audio clips of 30 seconds or less. Detection systems can also vary in accuracy as generation technology changes. For these reasons, detector results should be treated as evidence rather than absolute proof.

### Legal and Regulatory Approaches

Laws and regulations can establish requirements for the use of synthetic media, such as requiring disclosure or restricting certain non-consensual and deceptive uses.

Legal rules can create consequences for harmful behavior, but they cannot prevent every synthetic artifact from being created or distributed. Technology also develops quickly, while laws and regulations may take longer to change. Legal requirements are therefore an important part of governance but cannot address every situation by themselves.

### Platform Policies

Social media and content platforms can establish rules for labeling, limiting, or removing deceptive synthetic media. Platforms can also provide reporting systems when users encounter misleading AI-generated content.

The limitation is that enforcement may not be consistent. Synthetic content can also move between platforms, and a label applied by one platform may not follow the content elsewhere. Platform policies can reduce exposure to harmful synthetic media, but their effectiveness depends heavily on enforcement.

### Professional and Organizational Norms

Organizations can establish their own rules for when employees may use synthetic media. These rules can require consent, disclosure, review, documentation, and approval before synthetic content is released.

Organizational policies can be more specific than broad laws because they can address the particular risks faced by an organization. However, policies still depend on employees understanding and following them. A policy cannot completely prevent intentional misuse or guarantee that content will remain properly labeled after it leaves the organization.

### Overall Assessment

No single mitigation completely solves the risks created by synthetic media. Disclosure provides transparency, provenance can provide information about origin, detection can identify suspicious media, laws can establish consequences, platforms can control distribution, and organizational policies can establish internal boundaries.

The strongest approach is therefore to use multiple safeguards together rather than relying on one system. My Task 6 experience especially demonstrated that realistic synthetic content can be convincing to a human listener while still being detectable by automated systems. Responsible synthetic-media use should account for both the capabilities and the limitations of these safeguards.
