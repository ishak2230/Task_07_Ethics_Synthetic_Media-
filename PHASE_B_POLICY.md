# Phase B — Synthetic Media Governance Policy

## Organizational Context

This policy is designed for a university communications office responsible for producing and distributing official university communications. This may include websites, social media posts, promotional materials, informational videos, campus announcements, and other public-facing content.

Synthetic media could provide useful tools for this office, such as generating narration for informational videos or creating accessible versions of university content. However, university communications also depend heavily on credibility and public trust. Synthetic media that falsely represents a university official, is insufficiently disclosed, or is distributed outside its intended context could create confusion and damage that trust.

For these reasons, this policy allows limited uses of synthetic media while establishing clear restrictions on how it may be created and distributed.

## 1. Purpose

The purpose of this policy is to establish requirements for the responsible creation, use, review, and distribution of synthetic media by the university communications office.

For this policy, synthetic media includes audio, video, images, or other media that are generated or substantially modified using artificial intelligence in ways that create realistic representations of human speech, appearance, or behavior.

## 2. Permitted Uses

Synthetic media may be used for:

- Narration of informational or educational materials using clearly synthetic or properly licensed voices.
- Generic or synthetic avatars in instructional or informational videos.
- Drafts, prototypes, and internal demonstrations that are not publicly distributed.
- Accessibility or translation purposes when the use is reviewed and clearly disclosed.
- Creative or promotional content when audiences are clearly informed that synthetic media was used.

All public-facing synthetic media must follow the consent, disclosure, provenance, and review requirements described in this policy.

## 3. Prohibited Uses

The university communications office may not use synthetic media to:

- Make a real person appear to say or do something they did not say or do without documented consent.
- Fabricate statements from university administrators, faculty, staff, students, alumni, or other identifiable individuals.
- Create deceptive emergency, safety, disciplinary, financial, or official university announcements.
- Remove or intentionally hide required AI-generated disclosures.
- Impersonate another organization or individual.
- Produce synthetic media intended to mislead an audience about its origin or authenticity.
- Use a person's voice, likeness, or other identifiable characteristics after consent has been denied or revoked.

These uses are prohibited even when an employee believes the intended purpose is harmless.

## 4. Consent Workflow

When synthetic media uses the voice, likeness, or identifiable characteristics of a real person, documented consent must be obtained before production begins.

The consent process must include:

- A clear explanation of how the person's voice, likeness, or other characteristics will be used.
- A description of where the synthetic content will be distributed.
- Written confirmation that the individual agrees to the use.
- A record of the consent maintained by the communications office.
- A defined period for which the consent remains valid.

Individuals may revoke consent for future uses. After consent is revoked, the university may not create new synthetic media using that person's identity. Existing content should be reviewed to determine whether it should remain publicly available.

## 5. Disclosure Standards

All public-facing synthetic media must clearly disclose the use of AI.

For video, a visible label such as **"AI-Generated Synthetic Media"** must appear in the content or immediately alongside it.

For audio-only content, a spoken disclosure should be included when practical, and the description or accompanying text must identify the audio as AI-generated.

Disclosures must be easy for an ordinary viewer or listener to understand. Employees may not intentionally hide disclosures in fine print or use vague wording that makes the synthetic nature of the content unclear.

When possible, disclosures should be incorporated directly into the artifact so they are less likely to become separated when the content is shared.

## 6. Provenance Requirements

The communications office should preserve available information about how synthetic media was created.

For each public synthetic-media artifact, the office should maintain an internal record containing:

- The AI tool or tools used.
- The original source material.
- The date the artifact was generated.
- The employee responsible for creating it.
- Documentation of required consent.
- The final approved version of the artifact.

When supported by the tools being used, content credentials, metadata, or watermarking should also be preserved.

Provenance information should not replace visible disclosure because metadata may be removed when content is downloaded, edited, or reposted.

## 7. Review and Approval

Public-facing synthetic media must be reviewed before publication.

The employee creating the content may not be the only person responsible for approving it. At least one designated communications supervisor must review the final artifact.

The reviewer must confirm that:

- The information presented is accurate.
- Required consent has been obtained.
- The synthetic-media disclosure is present and understandable.
- The content does not misleadingly represent a real person.
- Required documentation has been retained.
- The proposed use complies with this policy.

Higher-risk uses involving university leadership, sensitive subjects, emergency information, or potentially misleading representations should be escalated for additional review or refused.

## 8. Incident Response

If synthetic media produced by the university is misused, altered, misattributed, or distributed without its required disclosure, the communications office must respond promptly.

The office should:

1. Preserve copies and documentation of the affected content.
2. Determine how the content was altered or misrepresented.
3. Notify appropriate university leadership.
4. Publish a correction or clarification when audiences may have been misled.
5. Contact relevant platforms when removal, correction, or labeling is appropriate.
6. Review whether the incident resulted from a weakness in the current policy or approval process.
7. Document the incident and any corrective actions taken.

## 9. Refusal to Produce Synthetic Media

The communications office must refuse a synthetic-media project when the risks cannot be reasonably controlled.

This includes situations where:

- Required consent cannot be obtained.
- The proposed content could reasonably be mistaken for an authentic statement from a real person.
- Appropriate disclosure cannot be maintained.
- The accuracy of the underlying information cannot be verified.
- The content involves deceptive impersonation.
- The project creates unnecessary risk to university credibility, safety, or public trust.

The fact that synthetic media is technically possible or inexpensive to produce is not sufficient justification for using it.

## 10. Policy Limitations and Stress Test

This policy can reduce the risks associated with synthetic media, but it cannot eliminate them completely. Its effectiveness depends partly on employees understanding the rules and acting in good faith.

One limitation is that disclosures may not remain attached to synthetic content after publication. A person could download a university video, remove or crop the AI-generated label, and repost the content somewhere else. The university can require disclosure on the original artifact, but it cannot completely control how that artifact is modified after distribution.

The policy also depends on employees correctly recognizing when consent or additional review is required. A well-intentioned employee might believe that a particular use of a person's likeness is minor enough that formal consent is unnecessary. Employees might also use a new AI tool without realizing that it handles provenance, metadata, or synthetic-media labeling differently from previously approved tools.

A bad-faith actor presents a more serious limitation. Someone who intentionally wants to impersonate a university official or distribute deceptive synthetic media could simply ignore the policy. Internal rules alone cannot prevent a determined person from using publicly available AI tools outside university systems.

Detection technology also cannot provide a complete solution. In Task 6, Hive correctly identified a sample of my synthetic audio with 99.1% confidence, but this does not mean every synthetic artifact will be detected with the same accuracy. Generation and detection technologies can change, and detector results should not be treated as absolute proof.

Another limitation is that consent can become complicated over time. A person may initially approve a synthetic use of their voice or likeness and later change their mind. Even if the university stops future use and removes its own copies, previously downloaded or reposted versions may remain outside the university's control.

For these reasons, this policy should not be treated as a guarantee that synthetic media will never cause harm or confusion. Its purpose is to establish clear boundaries, require documentation and review, and reduce avoidable risks. The policy should also be reviewed periodically as synthetic-media technologies and organizational practices change.
